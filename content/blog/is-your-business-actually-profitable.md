## Understanding business profitability

Profitability asks whether the business keeps anything after the costs attached to its sales. Start with four figures, and keep them separate.

**Revenue** is the value of goods or services sold during the period. It is not the same as cash in the bank. A sale on credit is revenue when it is earned, even if the customer has not paid yet.

**Direct costs** are the costs of the items sold, such as materials or wholesale goods. Subtract those from revenue to get **gross profit**.

**Operating expenses** are the costs of running the business for the period, such as rent, utilities, paid staff, and insurance. Subtract those from gross profit to get **operating profit**.

**Net profit** goes one step further. It subtracts non-operating items that apply to the period, such as interest and income tax. [Understanding Business Profit Margins](/blog/understanding-business-profit-margins) shows how each of those results becomes a percentage of revenue.

A positive net profit means the accounting result for the period is a gain. It does not mean that amount is sitting in the checking account.

## Profit versus cash flow

**Cash flow** is money moving in and out of the business. Accounting profit can be positive while cash falls, because several common events affect one and not the other.

- **Accounts receivable.** A credit sale increases revenue immediately. Cash arrives only when the customer pays.
- **Inventory purchases.** Paying for goods that are still on the shelf uses cash now. Those goods become a direct cost only when they are sold.
- **Loan principal payments.** The principal reduces the loan balance. It is not an operating expense. The interest portion is the expense.
- **Equipment purchases.** Buying a machine uses cash when you pay for it. If the example does not record depreciation that month, the purchase does not reduce that month's profit.
- **Payment timing.** Rent, payroll, taxes, and supplier bills can leave the account before the related sales are collected.

A useful review lists the profit result and then lists the cash items that profit left out.

## Understanding break-even

Break-even is the sales level where contribution covers the costs that do not change with each unit sold.

**Variable cost** changes with each unit. **Fixed cost**, for a simple monthly estimate, stays the same across the sales volumes you are comparing. **Contribution margin** per unit is the selling price minus variable cost per unit. Required unit volume is fixed cost divided by contribution margin per unit.

That volume is a planning threshold, not a forecast of demand. The [Break-Even Calculator](/calculators/break-even-calculator) applies the same relationship to your own price, variable cost, and fixed cost.

## Worked business example

The figures below describe one hypothetical month for a small product business. They are a teaching example, not an industry average. The only variable cost is $18 per unit sold. Operating expenses are treated as fixed for this comparison. The income-tax line is an assumed 20% of profit after interest, not a calculated tax return. No depreciation is recorded, and the owner is not paid a separate wage.

The business sells **400 units** at **$50**.

- Revenue: 400 × $50 = **$20,000**.
- Direct costs: 400 × $18 = **$7,200**.
- Gross profit: $20,000 − $7,200 = **$12,800**.
- Gross margin: $12,800 ÷ $20,000 = **64%**.

Operating expenses are rent $2,400, utilities and software $400, paid staff $3,200, and insurance and other overhead $600. Those total **$6,600**.

- Operating profit is **$6,200** ($12,800 − $6,600).
- Operating margin is $6,200 ÷ $20,000 = **31%**.

Interest expense is **$300**. Profit after interest is $6,200 − $300 = **$5,900**. The assumed tax is 20% × $5,900 = **$1,180**.

- Net profit is **$4,720** ($5,900 − $1,180).
- Net margin is $4,720 ÷ $20,000 = **23.6%**.

Contribution margin per unit is $50 − $18 = **$32**. Theoretical operating break-even, before interest and tax, is $6,600 ÷ $32 = **206.25 units**. Selling 206 whole units produces 206 × $32 = $6,592 of contribution, which is $8 short of the $6,600 operating expenses, so 206 units are not sufficient. The minimum whole-unit sales required is **207 units**. Theoretical break-even revenue is $6,600 ÷ 0.64 = **$10,312.50**. Revenue at 207 units is 207 × $50 = **$10,350**. At 400 units, the month is above that operating threshold.

Cash tells a different story. Customers still owe **$3,000**, so cash collected is **$17,000**. The business also pays **$1,500** for inventory that remains unsold, so cash paid for goods is $7,200 + $1,500 = **$8,700**. It pays the $6,600 of operating expenses, $300 of interest, an **$800** loan principal payment, a **$2,000** equipment purchase, and the $1,180 tax.

Cash paid is $8,700 + $6,600 + $300 + $800 + $2,000 + $1,180 = **$19,580**. Cash collected minus cash paid is $17,000 − $19,580, so cash decreased by **$2,580**.

The $4,720 net profit and the $2,580 cash decrease can both be true. The bridge is the $3,000 still uncollected, the $1,500 added to inventory, the $800 principal payment, and the $2,000 equipment purchase: $4,720 − $3,000 − $1,500 − $800 − $2,000 = −$2,580.

## Warning signs of weak profitability

Use the same month to see how the result can turn. None of these signs proves the business will fail. Each one is a reason to look harder at the assumptions.

- **Declining margins.** If the price falls or the $18 unit cost rises, less of each sale remains after direct costs. Gross profit shrinks before operating expenses change.
- **Insufficient cash reserves.** This example begins the month able to absorb a $2,580 cash decrease. A business with little cash cannot absorb the same receivable, inventory, debt, and equipment timing.
- **Increasing operating costs.** The $6,600 operating total is fixed only for this comparison. A higher rent or payroll bill raises the sales volume required to cover it.
- **Dependence on unrealistic sales.** At 180 units, revenue is 180 × $50 = $9,000 and direct costs are 180 × $18 = $3,240. Contribution is $5,760. After $6,600 of operating expenses, operating profit is **−$840**. The month is below the 206.25-unit threshold. A plan that works only at 400 units is not yet a plan for a slower month.

## How to evaluate your business

Review one recent month with the same separation used above.

- Write down revenue, direct costs, operating expenses, interest, and taxes as separate lines.
- Calculate gross profit, operating profit, and net profit. The [Profit Margin Calculator](/calculators/profit-margin-calculator) compares those relationships from your own inputs.
- List cash movements that profit does not show: uncollected invoices, inventory purchases, loan principal, and equipment purchases.
- Compare unit sales with break-even. Then test a lower price, a higher unit cost, or a lower sales volume before you treat the base case as durable.
- For a side-by-side view of a base case and a weaker case, try the [Operating Outlook](/calculators/operating-outlook) calculator. Its scenarios are illustrations, not forecasts.

## Limitations

These examples are educational estimates. They use the assumptions stated above, including one product, one variable cost, fixed operating expenses, an assumed tax rate, no depreciation, and no owner wage. They do not replace professional financial or accounting advice.

Real statements classify costs differently, and tax, debt, and depreciation rules can change the result. A profitable month is not a guarantee of future profit or of cash available to withdraw.
