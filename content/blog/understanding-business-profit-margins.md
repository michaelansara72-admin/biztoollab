## What is a profit margin?

Profit margin measures how much of a business's revenue remains after a defined set of costs. It is usually expressed as a percentage. A margin is only meaningful when you know **which costs have been subtracted**.

Two businesses with identical revenue can have very different margins because their product costs, staffing, rent, marketing, financing, and tax situations differ.

## Gross profit margin

**Gross profit** is revenue minus the cost of goods sold, or COGS. Gross profit margin is gross profit divided by revenue, multiplied by 100.

Suppose a business earns **$50,000** in monthly revenue and incurs **$30,000** in direct costs of producing or acquiring what it sells.

- Revenue: $50,000.
- Cost of goods sold: $30,000.
- Gross profit: $20,000.
- Gross profit margin: $20,000 ÷ $50,000 × 100 = **40%**.

This means 40 cents of each revenue dollar remain after COGS, before operating expenses and other costs. Classification matters: different industries and accounting policies may treat some costs differently.

## Operating profit margin

Operating profit generally subtracts operating expenses such as salaries, rent, marketing, and administration, along with applicable operating charges, from gross profit. Operating margin is operating profit divided by revenue, multiplied by 100.

If the example business has **$12,000** in additional operating expenses, operating profit is $8,000 and operating margin is **16%**.

Operating margin helps assess the performance of the core business before some non-operating items. Check how a financial statement defines operating income before comparing companies.

## Net profit margin

Net profit margin uses net income after the applicable expenses, interest, and taxes. If the example has another **$2,000** in interest and taxes combined, net profit becomes **$6,000**, and net margin is **12%**.

Gross margin of 40%, operating margin of 16%, and net margin of 12% are all correct for this illustrative business, but they answer different questions.

## Profit margin versus markup

Margin and markup are not interchangeable. If an item costs $60 and sells for $100, the gross profit is $40.

- Gross margin: $40 ÷ $100 = **40%**.
- Markup on cost: $40 ÷ $60 = **66.7%**.

Using markup when you intended to set a target margin can lead to underpricing. Always identify the denominator in the calculation.

## Is a higher margin always better?

Not necessarily. A business may earn a high percentage margin on relatively few sales, while another earns a lower percentage margin on a much larger volume. Inventory turnover, capital requirements, cash flow, customer retention, and risk all matter.

Margins also differ by industry and business model. Comparing a software subscription company directly with a grocery retailer without context is unlikely to be useful.

## How to improve margins responsibly

- **Understand direct costs:** Review supplier prices, waste, returns, and production efficiency.
- **Review pricing:** Test whether your value proposition supports a different price without assuming sales volume will stay constant.
- **Manage overhead:** Separate essential spending from costs that do not contribute enough value.
- **Track trends:** Compare the same margin definition over time rather than switching definitions between reports.
- **Protect quality:** Cutting costs that damage customer experience can hurt long-term profitability.

## Connect margin analysis to break-even planning

Margin tells you what remains from revenue after specified costs; break-even analysis estimates the sales volume required to cover costs. Together, they can reveal whether your current pricing and expense structure are sustainable.
